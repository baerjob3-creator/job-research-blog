# Ghost Particles May Decide Whether Dying Stars Explode — or Vanish Into Black Holes

**Subtitle:** Copenhagen simulations of 195 collapsing stars show that neutrino flavor conversion can tip massive stars toward failed supernovas — and help explain why the universe seems short on explosions.

---

When a massive star dies, almost everything that matters is invisible. Nuclear fuel runs out. The iron core implodes. Protons and electrons crush into neutrons. And in a fraction of a second, the star dumps about **99% of the released gravitational energy** not into light, shock waves, or fireworks — but into a flood of **neutrinos**, the ghost particles that barely notice ordinary matter.

That flood is not a side show. It is the engine. Neutrino heating behind a stalled shock can revive the blast and leave a neutron star. Fail to heat enough, and the star collapses into a black hole with little or no optical bang.

On September 20, 2026, *Space.com* amplified a *Physical Review D* study that puts a sharper edge on that story: the way neutrinos **change flavor** — electron, muon, or tau — may decide which fate wins. Authors **Mariam Gogilashvili** and **Irene Tamborra** of the University of Copenhagen’s Niels Bohr Institute simulated the collapse of **195 stellar progenitors** from **9 to 120 solar masses**, folding in a schematic treatment of neutrino flavor conversion. Their conclusion is blunt. Flavor change can make dying massive stars **significantly less likely to explode** and **more likely to form black holes** — especially in the awkward middleweight band of **16–30 solar masses**, where standard runs often succeed and nature often seems to vanish.

“Neutrinos are not a side detail in supernovas,” Gogilashvili told *Space.com*. “They carry away about 99% of the energy released when the core collapses, and a small change in how they behave can decide the fate of the whole star.”

## Three flavors, one deadly hinge

Neutrinos come in three flavors, named for the charged partners they couple to: electron, muon, and tau. Since 1998 — work that later earned a Nobel Prize — physicists have known these particles **oscillate**, swapping identities as they travel. Electron-flavor neutrinos and antineutrinos are the ones that deposit energy through charged-current interactions behind the shock. Muon and tau flavors interact far more weakly with ordinary matter. Swap flavors in the dense heart of a dying star, and you can change how efficiently the “heating battery” runs.

For years, modelers treated flavor conversion as a curiosity that would not matter much where the explosion decision is made. Then dense-neutrino physics got stranger. When neutrinos are packed tightly enough to interact with *each other*, collective flavor conversion can fire up near the proto-neutron star — not only far away in vacuum. That is the regime Gogilashvili and Tamborra lean on: not a slow textbook oscillation across light-years, but a **schematic instantaneous flavor equipartition** triggered below a chosen baryon-density threshold, spanning from near the neutrinosphere out toward the stalled shock.

They ran the open-source, spherically symmetric code **GR1D** with energy-dependent neutrino transport, the SFHo equation of state, and a reduced-dimensionality turbulence model (STIR) to stand in for convection that full 3-D codes resolve more honestly. Flavor conversion is applied shortly after bounce in regions below density cutoffs from **10⁹ to 10¹³ g/cm³**. Success means the shock expands past **1,000 km**; otherwise the model counts as a failed supernova.

## The numbers that flip the sky’s ledger

Without flavor conversion, **25.6%** of their models fail to explode — about **27%** when weighted by a Salpeter initial mass function, in the ballpark of observational hints that a large share of massive-star deaths may be optically dark or faint.

Turn flavor conversion on, and the failed fraction climbs. IMF-weighted failure rates run from **47.6%** at the lowest density threshold they tested to **88.3%** at the highest. Raw model failure fractions go even higher in some cases (up to **96.4%** of the simulated set). The paper’s own table is the cleanest scoreboard: deeper, denser conversion generally means **more cooling into heavy-lepton flavors**, less efficient electron-flavor heating, and more black-hole endings.

Tamborra’s quote to *Space.com* lands the surprise: stars between **16 and 30 solar masses** — “many of which explode comfortably in our standard simulations” — become **particularly sensitive**. A large number of them fail once flavor conversion is included.

That matters because astronomy has two long-running headaches in exactly this mass neighborhood. The **red-supergiant problem**: identified Type IIP supernova progenitors seem to cut off below the heaviest red supergiants we see in the wild. The **supernova-rate problem**: the cosmic core-collapse rate inferred from explosions sits systematically below the rate you would expect from star formation. More failed explosions in the 16–30 solar-mass window is not a full solution — but it is a physically motivated way to ease both tensions without inventing a new class of stars.

## Lighter neutron stars when the blast still wins

Flavor conversion does not only kill explosions. When conversion happens near the stalled shock in a way that **accelerates shock revival**, successful models leave **less massive** compact remnants. Earlier revival means less post-bounce accretion onto the proto-neutron star. The paper reports baryonic remnant masses for successful explosions spanning roughly **1.2–1.9 solar masses**, systematically lower with flavor conversion than without — after the usual baryonic-to-gravitational correction, a better match to the observed neutron-star mass peak around **1.2–1.4 solar masses**.

In other words: the same microphysics that can erase a supernova can also shave the survivors. That dual effect is why the authors call flavor conversion a **fundamental ingredient** in predicting neutron-star and black-hole populations, not an optional garnish on the neutrino transport module.

## What this is — and what it is not

It is **not** a claim that every 20-solar-mass star is doomed, that black holes suddenly outnumber neutron stars by a known factor, or that 3-D supernova theory is finished. The treatment of flavor conversion is **schematic** — instantaneous equipartition below a density cut, not a fully dynamical, multi-angle quantum-kinetic evolution. The hydrodynamics are 1-D+ with a turbulence surrogate. High failure fractions at the densest thresholds (**~74–88%** IMF-weighted) sit in tension with some observational caps; the authors say so plainly and flag the need for subgrid models that evolve in time and space inside multi-dimensional simulations. Nuclear equation of state, progenitor set, and missing proto-neutron-star convection can all move the percentages.

It **is** the first broad survey — across nearly two hundred progenitors — arguing that neutrino flavor conversion systematically reshapes which stars explode, which fail, and how heavy the leftover neutron stars are. Peer-reviewed acceptance in *Physical Review D* (accepted August 5, 2026; DOI **10.1103/pz3y-3lv5**), with an earlier arXiv posting (**2605.16504**), and a same-day public science wave on September 20, 2026.

## Ghosts with veto power

Supernovas built the periodic table’s heavy half and seeded the dust that becomes planets. Black holes rewrite spacetime and dominate gravitational-wave catalogs. The hinge between those outcomes has always been energy transport in a region denser than a nucleus and hotter than anything on Earth. Gogilashvili and Tamborra’s contribution is to show — in a controlled, if idealized, simulation campaign — that **identity swaps among neutrinos** can tip that hinge.

Future work they flag is the obvious next chapter: more realistic flavor physics that starts and evolves as the star dies, inside 3-D cores that already strain the world’s supercomputers. Until then, the public takeaway is sharp enough for a Sunday night share. The universe’s most plentiful messengers may also be its quietest executioners — deciding, particle by particle, whether a dying star gets a fireworks display or simply disappears.
