# They Put a Superconductor in a Dark Cavity — Vacuum Fluctuations Raised Its Critical Temperature 5.4%

**Subtitle:** USTC, Shanghai Jiao Tong, and MIT’s Frank Wilczek report in *Nature* the first experimental boost to superconductivity from engineered quantum vacuum fluctuations — no laser drive, no chemical doping, just a terahertz “dark cavity” around layered NbSe₂.

---

Empty space is a terrible brand name for what physicists actually mean. In quantum electrodynamics, a vacuum is not a void. It is a restless background of electromagnetic fluctuations in which virtual particles flicker in and out of existence. Those fluctuations help explain the Lamb shift, spontaneous emission, and the Casimir force between closely spaced plates. For decades they were treated as scenery — the stage on which real experiments happen.

On **August 19, 2026**, *Nature* published evidence that the stage can become an actor. Zheyan Wang, Gabriel Cardoso, Liu Yang, and collaborators led by **Changgan Zeng** and **Guanghui Cheng** at the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC), with theory from **Qing-Dong Jiang** (Shanghai Jiao Tong University) and Nobel laureate **Frank Wilczek** (MIT), embedded the layered superconductor **niobium diselenide (NbSe₂)** in a near-terahertz split-ring resonator they call a **dark cavity**. Inside that cavity — with **no light shining on the sample and no energy actively pumped in** — the material’s superconducting critical temperature rose by as much as **5.4%**, and near the transition its critical current and critical magnetic field jumped as well (DOI **10.1038/s41586-026-11037-x**).

That is the first peer-reviewed demonstration that vacuum fluctuations, amplified by a cavity, can **enhance** superconductivity rather than merely leave it alone — or, in some earlier cavity experiments on other platforms, suppress it.

## Why “dark” matters

Ordinary cavity-QED tricks often involve shining microwaves or terahertz light into a resonator and watching matter respond. This experiment’s claim is subtler. The cavity is **dark**: it reshapes the electromagnetic environment so that vacuum fluctuations are strongly amplified — Physics World notes factors of a hundred or more are typical for resonant structures — without an external drive field.

NbSe₂ is a well-characterized transition-metal dichalcogenide superconductor, convenient because its few-layer behavior is mapped and its transition is sharp enough to compare regions of the **same flake**. The team partially embedded the flake in the split-ring structure, then measured resistance versus temperature **inside** versus **outside** the cavity. Same crystal. Same fabrication run. Different electromagnetic bath.

The headline number: up to a **5.4%** increase in critical temperature (**T<sub>c</sub>**) for a **six-layer** NbSe₂ device. Near T<sub>c</sub>, critical current and critical field rose substantially — exactly where a small shift in the free-energy landscape of the superconducting state should matter most.

## Ruling out the boring explanations

Extraordinary claims about empty space invite ordinary rebuttals: strain from mounting the flake, sample inhomogeneity, fabrication damage, dielectric clutter, metallic screening from the resonator itself. The USTC team’s answer was a control campaign — cavity geometry and characteristic frequency, material thickness, dielectrics, metallic strips — designed to knock those alternatives down.

The smoking gun was **resonance**. Enhancement of superconductivity peaked when the cavity’s characteristic frequency lined up with low-energy superconducting fluctuations. A frequency-dependent, peak-like response tied to the cavity’s photonic properties is hard to fake with a static strain story. As Zeng put it in the Chinese Academy of Sciences release: that resonant fingerprint is strong evidence that the superconducting state is coupling to dark-cavity modes.

## Vacuumtronics, in one sentence

Jiang’s group had already been pushing a research program they brand **vacuumtronics**: engineer the vacuum environment so it regulates electronic and photonic behavior the way doping, pressure, or gating usually do. Wilczek and Jiang’s theoretical framing, in a Ginzburg–Landau picture, is that the superconducting condensate can exchange **virtual photons** with the cavity modes. That hybridization lowers the energy of the superconducting state relative to the normal state — which is another way of saying superconductivity gets stronger.

Wilczek’s quote travels well for a reason: “In most practical physics, the vacuum serves merely as the passive stage on which phenomena play out. This work shows that the background itself can become an actor — engineered to strengthen superconductivity and reshape the behavior of quantum matter.”

## What this is — and what it is not

It is **not** a room-temperature superconductor. NbSe₂ still lives in the cryogenic neighborhood of a few kelvin; a 5.4% relative bump does not move the field into liquid-nitrogen territory overnight. It is **not** a power-grid miracle or a Maglev redesign. It is **not** proof that every superconducting qubit will suddenly run hotter because you parked it next to a split ring.

It **is** a proof-of-principle that a noninvasive knob — cavity-shaped vacuum fluctuations — can push T<sub>c</sub>, critical current, and critical field in a bulk-ish condensed-matter system without chemistry, pressure cells, or laser drives. Cheng’s emphasis in Physics World coverage is the point: the cavity is dark; the effect is attributed to ever-present quantum electromagnetic fluctuations, not to photons you paid for with a laser budget.

For quantum technology, the near-term fantasy is narrower and more plausible than “fix ambient superconductivity.” Superconducting circuits, sensors, and quantum devices already live inside engineered electromagnetic environments. If vacuum engineering can nudge coherence windows, critical currents, or operating margins without adding heat or chemical disorder, that is a materials-control tool worth iterating — even when today’s percentage gains are modest.

## How Sunday science should hold the number

Five-point-four percent is both the shareable headline and the honesty filter. The team is explicit that they want larger effects via better cavities and material stacks. Independent groups will need to reproduce the resonant enhancement, map which superconductors respond, and stress-test whether “vacuumtronics” survives outside NbSe₂’s convenient few-layer platform. Earlier theory and other cavity experiments had often predicted or reported **suppression** of superconducting properties; an enhancement result therefore has to clear a higher bar of skepticism, which is why the controls and the resonant peak matter as much as the percentage.

Still: for a century, textbooks treated vacuum fluctuations as subtle corrections. Casimir forces bend mirrors. Lamb shifts tweak spectral lines. Now a dark cavity has nudged a macroscopic quantum phase — superconductivity — in a measurable, frequency-tuned way. Empty space did not invent a new superconductor. It reminded the field that the electromagnetic background is part of the Hamiltonian, and that engineers who can shape that background may have inherited a new control axis.

No light in. No doping recipe. Same flake, two environments. A 5.4% T<sub>c</sub> lift where the cavity modes match the condensate’s soft fluctuations. Not a fridge you can unplug. A peer-reviewed first for vacuum-enhanced superconductivity — and a Sunday invitation to stop calling the vacuum empty.
