# They Made Ammonia From Air, Seawater, Iron Salts, and a UV Lamp — No Haber-Bosch Furnace

**Subtitle:** Université de Bordeaux chemists report in *Nature Chemistry* an artificial photosynthetic route that couples nitrogen reduction to water oxidation — quantum yield 5% at room temperature and pressure, 10.8% under pressurized N₂, and it still works in unpurified seawater or ambient air.

---

Modern civilization runs on a reaction most people never think about. Roughly half of the nitrogen in a human body, on average, has passed through an industrial ammonia plant. The Haber–Bosch process — high temperature, high pressure, fossil-derived hydrogen — feeds the world. It also burns a large share of global industrial energy and, by the paper’s own framing, accounts for about **1.8% of greenhouse-gas emissions**. For a century the trade-off was accepted because the alternative was hunger.

On **August 25, 2026**, *Nature Chemistry* published a different kind of answer. **Doha Zidouhia**, **Thierry Tassaing**, **Nicolas Penin**, **Etienne Grau**, and **Dario M. Bassani** at Université de Bordeaux / CNRS report light-driven production of ammonia from dinitrogen in water at **room temperature and pressure**, using **inexpensive iron(II) salts**, ultraviolet-C light, and a molybdenum catalyst — and they show the same chemistry still runs in **unpurified seawater** or under **ambient air** (DOI **10.1038/s41557-026-02218-2**). A News & Views piece by Jens Uhlig followed on **September 17, 2026**, framing the result as a promising new pathway for one of chemistry’s most important reactions.

This is not another “hydrogen from seawater” leaf story. The molecule that matters here is **NH₃** — fertilizer feedstock, chemical building block, and a candidate hydrogen carrier — made by **fixing atmospheric nitrogen** while **oxidizing water**.

## What “photosynthetic” means in this paper

Many lab recipes for photochemical ammonia are **photocatalytic** in a thermodynamic sense: they dump energetic sacrificial reductants (amines, alcohols, exotic metal reagents) downhill into ammonia and call it a day. The Bordeaux team’s claim, sharpened in their related *Coordination Chemistry Reviews* essay, is that this system is **photosynthetic**: the net chemistry is **endergonic** by **329.2 kJ mol⁻¹**. Light does real thermodynamic work. Water is the terminal electron donor. Nitrogen is reduced. Oxygen is the stoichiometric counterpart of water oxidation.

The measured headline efficiencies:

- **Photochemical quantum yield 5.0%** for ammonia formation in water at normal temperature and pressure.
- **10.8%** quantum yield once they push dinitrogen pressure to overcome nitrogen’s miserable solubility in water.
- **7.6%** calculated light-to-chemical energy conversion efficiency for the endergonic N₂ + H₂O → NH₃ + O₂ stoichiometry.

Those are not Haber–Bosch plant numbers. They are laboratory quantum yields that put a homogeneous, aqueous, water-coupled nitrogen-fixation photochemistry into a range the field has chased for years without needing sacrificial SmI₂ or sodium amalgams as the electron bank.

## The iron trick

The architecture is almost provocatively simple. Under **254-nm UVC**, aqueous **iron(II)** salts generate **solvated electrons**. Those electrons reduce a **molybdenum** catalyst of the Nishibayashi family (a Mo–PCP pincer complex), which then does the hard work of activating N₂ and stepping it toward ammonia. The oxidized **iron(III)** does not sit as waste: it also absorbs ultraviolet light, returns to Fe(II), and in the process drives **water oxidation**. Iron is both the photoelectron source and the shuttle that couples the two half-reactions.

Isotope experiments with **¹⁵N₂** and **H₂¹⁸O**, plus transient spectroscopy, support the net picture: water is oxidized; nitrogen is reduced; selectivity for N₂ over proton reduction is unexpectedly good because solvated electrons are quenched by the Mo catalyst on a diffusion-controlled timescale. First-author communication around publication noted that under ambient-air conditions the setup could reach on the order of **0.28 g L⁻¹** ammonia after **19 hours** — a lab concentration, not a warehouse, but a concrete “room air + lamp” number.

Salt and oxygen do not kill the reaction. That is why **natural seawater** and **ambient air** appear in the abstract rather than as afterthoughts. Haber–Bosch plants demand purified feeds and megawatt thermal envelopes. This flask asks for light, iron, a Mo catalyst, and wet nitrogen.

## What this is not

It is **not** a drop-in replacement for Haber–Bosch. Global ammonia production is measured in hundreds of millions of tonnes per year; this is a peer-reviewed demonstration of a photosynthetic mechanism and quantum yields under controlled lamps. It is **not** sunlight-ready in the form reported: the photochemistry leans on **UVC (254 nm)**, not the visible spectrum that hits a farmer’s field. Scaling will need either UVC engineering (already common in water treatment) or a redesign that moves the iron photochemistry toward longer wavelengths. It is **not** catalyst-free — molybdenum still sits at the nitrogen-activation heart — and pressurized N₂ helps because chemistry cannot invent dissolved nitrogen that water refuses to hold.

Hold those caveats in the same breath as the shareable line. The shareable line is still extraordinary: **room-temperature, aqueous, water-coupled artificial photosynthetic nitrogen fixation that tolerates seawater and air**, with a **5%** quantum yield at 1 bar and **10.8%** when solubility is forced.

## Why Sunday readers should care

Fertilizer chemistry is planetary infrastructure. Every percentage point of fossil ammonia that can eventually be displaced by light-driven, water-coupled routes is a climate and food-security story at once. Prior homogeneous Mo systems often leaned on harsh sacrificial reductants; semiconductor photocatalysts often posted tiny apparent quantum yields. Bordeaux’s Fe/Mo photochemistry occupies a middle lane that looks chemically honest: iron for electrons, molybdenum for N₂, water for both protons and the oxidative half, light for the thermodynamic lift.

Uhlig’s News & Views puts the aspiration cleanly — iron salts and a molybdenum catalyst driving photosynthetic reduction of atmospheric nitrogen to ammonia with water as the terminal electron donor. The next experiments write themselves: longer-wavelength iron photosensitizers, continuous-flow reactors that recycle Mo, independent labs checking seawater and air runs, and an honest life-cycle comparison against green-hydrogen Haber–Bosch. None of that is promised by an August paper. All of it is unlocked by one.

A century ago, Haber and Bosch taught humanity to squeeze nitrogen with heat and pressure. Zidouhia, Grau, Bassani, and colleagues just showed that under a UVC lamp, iron and molybdenum can ask nitrogen the same question more gently — in water, even seawater, even from the air in the room — and get ammonia back with a quantum yield you can put in a headline without inventing a factory.

No fossil hydrogen cylinder. No 400 °C pressure vessel in the story’s core. Air or seawater. Iron salts. A Mo catalyst. A UV lamp. Five percent quantum yield at ambient pressure; ten-point-eight when they squeeze more N₂ into solution. Not the end of Haber–Bosch. A peer-reviewed beginning for photosynthetic ammonia that finally couples the hard half-reaction to water oxidation in a flask Sunday science can explain.

- Uses UVC (254 nm), not ambient sunlight as reported.
- Distinct from post-07 seawater **hydrogen** artificial leaf — this is **ammonia / nitrogen fixation**.
- Mo catalyst still required; 10.8% QY needs elevated N₂ for solubility.
